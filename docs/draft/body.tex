\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A growing class of language-model systems promises to test scientific
hypotheses against data: given a claim and a dataset, they choose a statistical
method, run it and report a conclusion. Benchmarks for this ability
\cite{miao2026fisher,zhu2024statqa,zhu2026statabench} score the final answer ---
a $p$-value, a reject/fail-to-reject decision --- and hand the system the
relevant data already assembled. In most real settings that is the easy half.
Consider the claim \emph{``after the Pfizer vaccine, headache tends to precede
fatigue''} against a corpus of annotated adverse-event reports. Before any test
can run, a system has to decide \emph{which reports count}: those in which both
events occur at different stages (a report where they co-occur witnesses
neither order), \emph{what ``no effect'' means} (a 50/50 split of the ordered
reports), and \emph{which test applies} (a one-sided exact binomial with a
minimum-count floor). Each of those decisions is a stipulation about what the
sentence means for this corpus, each can be wrong independently of the others,
and a verdict can be right while all of them are wrong.

This paper makes that chain of decisions the object of evaluation. We release
\sys{} (Staged Temporal-statistical Reasoning Assessment), a benchmark and evaluation framework for temporal--statistical claims over MedTempo, a corpus of
3{,}166 vaccine adverse-event reports whose clinical course is annotated as a
\emph{leveled order} of events. Every instance carries not only its gold
verdict --- \emph{supported}, \emph{refuted} or \emph{insufficient}, the last
sub-typed --- but the gold of every stage that produced it: the structured plan
(events, quantity, denominator rule, null, test), the exact set of report
identifiers in the denominator and numerator, and the exact $p$-values. Gold is
computed mechanically from the annotations, validated by six families of
invariants, and reproduced to 1.000 by a deterministic oracle, so a system's
intermediate outputs can be scored, not only its answer.

We pair the benchmark with an \emph{instrumented reference pipeline}: a plan
stage, a retrieval stage and a deterministic test stage, each with an explicit
output contract and a bounded verify-and-repair loop. The pipeline is a
measuring instrument as much as a method: its stages can be filled with gold,
its prompts can state or withhold the corpus conventions, and its plan can be
handed to the next stage either as prose or as a contract that the harness
resolves. That lets us separate two things that end-to-end evaluation
conflates --- the \emph{information} a system is told and the
\emph{architecture} it is given --- and ask what each is worth.

Three questions organise the experiments and three findings answer them, all
on a 1{,}500-claim test set that no prompt decision was made on, with five
models from the 2024 open generation to the 2026 frontier in every model
slot. First, \emph{the stated conventions are the information the task
measures}: with them in the prompt, Claude Opus~5.5 and GPT-5.6 verify 1.000
and 0.984 of the claims end to end with every denominator exact; without them
the same models fall to 0.88 and get a fifth of their denominators wrong.
Second, \emph{for open models the plan contract is what makes the verdict
stand on the right evidence}: a 2024 32B pair goes from 0.575 end to end to
0.810 with the contract and the verifier, the 2026 gemma4-31B from 0.859 to
0.981 --- within 0.02 of the frontier at zero API cost --- and the share of
right verdicts that rest on the exact denominator rises from 40--86\% to
86--97\%. Third, \emph{the denominator is where systems fail, and verdict
accuracy hides it}: a published agentic baseline reaches 0.605, within 0.03 of
the end-to-end control, while deciding 58\% of the claims the data cannot
decide; handed the exact counts, the open models still return the wrong
verdict on a quarter to a third of claims.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A benchmark with gold at every stage.} \sys{} poses
temporal--statistical claims in 26 groups and seven claim families over three
cohort tiers, released as a 200-instance development set and a 1{,}500-claim
fact-disjoint test set (plus a 180-claim single-report case study), each
instance with its structured plan, its exact evidence sets, its exact
$p$-values and a three-decision verdict with sub-typed abstention, over
pseudonymised identifiers (\S\ref{sec:dataset}).
\item \textbf{A generation process that makes the conventions part of the
task.} Claims are enumerated from the annotated timelines by templates; every
gold stage is computed mechanically, screened against permutation nulls,
validated by six families of invariants and reproduced by a deterministic
oracle; and the corpus stipulations the labels depend on are shipped as text
that any system can be given, so ``conventions stated'' is a controlled
condition rather than a hidden assumption (\S\ref{sec:dataset}).
\item \textbf{A staged verification method.} A plan, retrieve, test
pipeline whose plan is handed to retrieval as a contract resolved by the
harness, with a bounded verify-and-repair loop at each stage and a
deterministic exact test; any stage can be filled with gold, so the loss of
each stage is measurable and information and architecture are separately
controllable (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A study of five models and two agentic systems} under three
research questions: what the stated conventions are worth, what the contract
adds and where the loss remains, and whether right verdicts rest on the right
evidence; with ablations of each stage and of the repair budget, case studies
of single reports and paraphrase, and an error analysis of the
\emph{insufficient} class (\S\ref{sec:experiments},
\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Statistical reasoning benchmarks.} StatQA \cite{zhu2024statqa}
scores whether a model knows which test applies; StatFormBench
\cite{wang2026statformbench} scores the formulation of a test family from a
description; StatABench \cite{zhu2026statabench} adds fixed tool functions and
scores the final answer. P-Bench and Fisher-R1 \cite{miao2026fisher} come
closest to our setting: 425 tasks in which an agent receives a hypothesis with
its null stated, a CSV of the relevant data and a glossary, writes R to compute
a $p$-value, and is scored on the decision and on the $p$-value's distance to
a reference. In all of these the data are already the relevant subset and the
null is given or trivial. The step we score first --- deciding which records a
statistic is computed over --- has no counterpart, and no intermediate stage
has gold.

\paragraph{Hypothesis-testing agents.} VERITAS \cite{veritas2026} is a
multi-agent co-scientist that formulates, analyses and interprets a hypothesis
with a deterministic evidence classifier, built for image-derived cohorts; LATCH
\cite{gim2026latch} runs LLM-chosen analyses through deterministic R pipelines
on EHR data with the method given; DiscoveryBench and BLADE
\cite{majumder2025discoverybench,gu2024blade} evaluate open-ended data-driven
discovery agents against rubrics. These systems share our plan-then-execute
shape and our insistence on auditable computation; none of them scores the
conditioning of the evidence, and none has hypotheses about the order of
events within a record. We run two of them (EHRAgent \cite{shi2024ehragent},
VERITAS) on our data as end-to-end baselines.

\paragraph{Clinical text-to-SQL and timelines.} EHRSQL \cite{lee2022ehrsql}
scores one query per question with an abstention option; EHRAgent
\cite{shi2024ehragent} solves EHR questions with a code-writing agent and
error-driven retries. The sentence-to-SQL baselines in \S\ref{sec:baselines}
follow that protocol over our schema. MedTempo and the CRAFT/TIMER line
\cite{craft2026,timer2025} extract clinical timelines from narratives; we start
where extraction ends, taking the expert-annotated timelines as given and
asking what can be claimed about them.

\paragraph{Positioning.} Table~\ref{tab:related} compares the closest work on
the dimensions that decide what can be claimed. Two things are not new: a
plan-then-execute loop with a deterministic statistics layer is the LATCH
architecture, and ``hypothesis and data in, method and $p$ out'' is P-Bench.
What is new is the combination of a model-formulated null executed
deterministically with gold at every stage, a scored eligibility rule, and
claims about event order with the conditioning semantics (first mention,
co-existence, ties) those claims carry.

\section{The \sys{} Benchmark}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Task}
\label{sec:task}


\paragraph{Input and output.} A \emph{record} is a claim in natural language
--- a question or a declarative sentence, in one of four registers --- about a
\emph{cohort} (the reports of one vaccine), a \emph{contrast} of two cohorts, or
one named \emph{report}; and the annotations: for every report, its vaccine
and the stage at which each event term is first mentioned. The system must
return a verdict in $\{\emph{supported}, \emph{refuted}, \emph{insufficient}\}$,
the last sub-typed as \emph{not-locatable} (no report qualifies),
\emph{sparse} (too few qualify) or \emph{underpowered} (enough qualify, neither
tail significant). It never sees report text.

\paragraph{What a verdict rests on.} For a cohort-tier claim the gold is
computed in three steps that are themselves released: (1) a \emph{plan} ---
the events named, the quantity compared, the \emph{denominator primitive}
(which reports count), the number of arms, the null (a fixed proportion, a bar
stated in the text, a rate derived from the data, or equality of two arms), the
tail and the test family; (2) the \emph{evidence} --- the identifiers of the
reports in each arm's denominator and numerator; (3) the exact test with a
per-family minimum-count floor and $\alpha = 0.05$ one-sided in the claim's
direction. \emph{Refuted} requires significance in the opposite direction, never
``failed to prove''. A report-tier claim is a single deterministic observation
over one report and carries no test.

\paragraph{Conventions are part of the task.} Whether a report with both
events at the same stage counts for ``precedes'', what ``typically'' means,
whether a tie for ``first'' leaves the denominator, what ``more often than their
separate rates would predict'' compares --- these are stipulations about the
corpus, not facts about the sentence, and a system that was never told them
cannot infer them. The benchmark therefore ships its conventions as a
situation-phrased text that any system may be given (\S\ref{sec:frames}), and
the experiments treat ``conventions stated'' as a controlled condition. The
claim-type \emph{mapping} --- which rule and which test go with which wording
--- is what we withhold: a system is scored on composing it.


\subsection{Source corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

MedTempo~\cite{medtempo2026} is a corpus of 3{,}166 VAERS adverse-event
reports for three COVID-19 vaccines, annotated with a \emph{leveled order}: a
single totally ordered chain of stages in which each stage holds the
MedDRA-coded events reported at that point of the clinical course
(Table~\ref{tab:corpus} in the appendix summarises the corpus). Two annotation
conventions are load-bearing: when a term recurs, its \emph{first} mention
fixes its stage; events at one stage are temporally indistinguishable
(\emph{co-exist}). The annotation, not the narrative, is both the benchmark's
ground truth and the systems' input.

\subsection{Hypothesis space}
\label{sec:space}

Twenty-six template groups span seven claim families at three scopes
(Table~\ref{tab:roster}). Four families concern order and co-occurrence ---
\emph{precedes}, \emph{co-occurs}, strict \emph{sequence} of three events,
\emph{first/last} among candidates --- and three, added to diversify the null,
concern \emph{position} (an event in the earlier half of a timeline),
\emph{proximity} (two events at consecutive stages) and \emph{threshold} (a
rate at least a stated bar). Scopes are a single report, one vaccine cohort,
and a contrast of two cohorts. Within a cohort, three groups are two-arm
contrasts: whether $B$ follows $A$ more often than it follows $C$; whether $A$
precedes $B$ more often than $C$ precedes $D$; and whether $A$ and $B$ occur
together more often than their separate rates predict (association). Each
group is realised in four registers (a canonical \textsc{Original} plus
\textsc{Research}, \textsc{Media} and \textsc{Clinical}) and two formats
(question and claim) by mechanical slot-filling with no model-generated
rewriting; answers are \textsc{Bool} or \textsc{Choice}.

\paragraph{Five kinds of null.} Order claims test a proportion against the
symmetry value $0.5$; argmax claims (``most common first'', ``most common
ordering'') test the named cell against its strongest competitor; two-arm
claims test the equality of two rates; threshold claims read their null off
the text (``in at least two thirds''); and the position and proximity-rate
claims use a null \emph{derived from the data} --- the structural early-rate of
the denominator reports, or the chance $2/L$ that two events of an $L$-stage
report are adjacent --- which no lookup table can supply. The last two kinds
are what make ``formulate the null'' a scored capability rather than a
classification.

\subsection{Gold labels}
\label{sec:gold}

\paragraph{Conditioning.} Each family fixes its denominator, and these
distinctions are the substantive content of the task. \emph{Precedes} counts
only reports where both events occupy distinct stages. \emph{Co-occurs} counts
all reports containing both, because co-existence is the event of interest.
\emph{Sequence} requires all three events at distinct stages. \emph{First/last}
requires every candidate present and drops tie reports entirely. Association
conditions on presence alone, comparing $P(B \mid A)$ against $P(B \mid \neg
A)$ over the whole cohort. \emph{Immediate} order counts reports where the two
events are at consecutive occupied stages; \emph{position} drops single-stage
reports and events on the exact middle stage.

\paragraph{Tests.} All tests are exact. Majority and bar claims use a one-sided
exact binomial against $p_0$; argmax claims use modal-versus-runner-up, an exact
binomial on the named cell against its strongest competitor; two-arm and
two-cohort contrasts use Fisher's exact test. No $\chi^2$ approximation
appears; the sole gate is a per-family minimum denominator ($n \geq 10$;
sequence $n \geq 5$), applied to both arms where two exist.

\paragraph{The three-decision rule and oriented statistics.} A claim is
\emph{supported} only when its own one-sided tail is significant at
$\alpha = 0.05$, \emph{refuted} only when the opposite tail is, and otherwise
\emph{insufficient} (\emph{underpowered}, \emph{sparse}: $0 < n <$ floor, or
\emph{not-locatable}: $n = 0$). Both tails are computed for every instance.
Every released statistic --- both $p$-values, the Wilson interval, Cohen's $h$
against the null actually used --- describes the claim as that instance asserts
it.

\subsection{Instance realisation and composition}
\label{sec:realization}

Every decisive core is posed \emph{both ways}: oriented with its effect
(\emph{supported}) and against it (\emph{refuted}), a mirror pair over
identical evidence, so a system answering \emph{supported} to both directions
of one fact is caught immediately. Direction-asking groups re-orient by
swapping slots; symmetric claims re-orient by authored negation, written into
the template rather than derived; threshold claims are tested per orientation
(both directions can be refuted) and carry no mirror. Each cohort group ships
equal thirds --- supported, refuted, insufficient --- balanced \emph{per group}
because template wording is fixed per group and a skewed group would teach a
text-shape-to-label shortcut; the insufficient third is spread over its
sub-types. \emph{Not-locatable} instances are minted deliberately from real
cohort vocabulary whose joint count is zero, so the claim appears answerable and
only querying the annotations reveals the empty denominator. Groups are capped
at 600 instances (200 per class) by a seeded draw that keeps mirror pairs
together; report groups ship two classes of 200.

\subsection{Validity screens}
\label{sec:screens}

No group ships on plausibility. Each is screened against a permutation null
matched to its claim shape at 2{,}000 rounds: a \emph{level shuffle}
(term-to-stage assignment permuted within each report, preserving report size,
stage-size multiset, term frequencies and co-occurrence) for order, position
and proximity groups; a degree-preserving \emph{edge swap} for association; a
\emph{label shuffle} for two-cohort contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:screens}). The
screening history is itself a result: seven candidate two-cohort shapes were
tested and one survived; a majority statistic was replaced after the screen
showed its text and its test answered different questions; and a rank-test
track was retired when the Mann--Whitney approximation proved anti-conservative
on tie-heavy ordinal data (${\sim}11\%$ of cores decisive under shuffled labels
at a nominal 5\%).

\subsection{Release, splits and leakage}
\label{sec:release}

\paragraph{Two files.} The questions file carries one row per rendering and
only the input fields (identifier, group, level, answer type, register, format,
text, options, cohort); the answers file carries one row per instance with the
gold verdict and sub-type, the oriented counts, both $p$-values, the test, the
floor, interval and effect size, and the \emph{evidence identifiers}. The writer
refuses to emit a gold field into the questions file. A group-independent
\texttt{fact\_id} keys every split, because answer-format twins pose the same
fact under two groups.

\paragraph{Pseudonymised identifiers.} Every report identifier a system can
see --- in the claim texts, in the released evidence and in the database --- is
a keyed permutation of the public VAERS identifier. VAERS is public and in
pretraining corpora; removing the lookup key removes the shortcut without
touching the annotations.

\paragraph{Splits.} The release is an evaluation benchmark with no training
split: a \emph{development set} of 200 instances (a seeded, class-balanced
draw over every group and tier, in both formats and all four registers) on
which every prompt and design decision in this paper was made, and a
\emph{test set} of 1{,}500 cohort- and multi-tier instances in claim form,
90 per group where the corpus allows it and every eligible instance where it
does not, class-balanced, mirror pairs and facts kept together, and
fact-disjoint from the development set. A further 180 single-report instances
(20 per report group) form a case study rather than part of the test set,
because that tier is read at 0.98--1.00 by every capable system. The full
enumerated pool (Table~\ref{tab:totals}) is released as supplementary
material with the generator, so larger or differently balanced draws can be
made with the same recipe. The test set was run once, after the prompts were
frozen.

\paragraph{Validation.} Six families of mechanical invariants --- evidence
arithmetic, set algebra, per-family conditioning re-derived from the leveled
orders, direction, verdict-against-tails including the floor, and independent
recomputation through a separate database path --- are checked over the whole
enumerated universe (56{,}138 instances). A deterministic oracle (reverse
template parser into the reference SQL compiler) reproduces every gold label of
the release: the harness is correct, whatever the text entails.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:framework}
\label{sec:method}

Our reference pipeline verifies a claim in three stages, plan, retrieve and
test, and is built so that each stage can be scored against its gold, filled
with gold, or told more or less about the corpus. This section gives the
stage contracts, the two prompt frames that control what the model is told,
and the verification loop; the systems it is compared against are in
\S\ref{sec:baselines}.

\subsection{Stage contracts}
\label{sec:contracts}

The reference pipeline decomposes verification into three stages with explicit
output contracts (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\textbf{S1 --- plan.} From the record (text, level, cohort) the model composes
a plan: \texttt{events}, \texttt{quantity}, \texttt{denominator} (a named
primitive: \emph{ordered\_pair}, \emph{both\_present},
\emph{all\_present\_unique\_first}, \emph{consecutive\_pair},
\emph{present\_offmid}, \emph{presence\_split}, \emph{single\_report}, \ldots),
\texttt{arms}, \texttt{null\_kind} with $p_0$ or a reference population,
\texttt{tail}, \texttt{test\_family} and a rule for the floor. The harness
derives the claim type from the plan by reverse lookup over the plan's fields;
a plan that names no known shape is \emph{underdetermined} and scored as
insufficient. Two normalisation folds are applied before lookup: a
consecutive-pair denominator implies the immediate-order quantity, and a
stated or derived null on a shape with no such twin keeps the shape and costs
one field.

\textbf{S2 --- retrieve.} For each arm the model writes one SQL statement over
the annotation database (two tables, two views) that returns \emph{one row per
report} with four flags: in the denominator, in the numerator, excluded, and an
optional cell label for argmax claims. The harness executes it in a read-only
sandbox and aggregates the rows into $(n, k)$ per arm together with the
identifier sets, so the evidence is auditable against the gold sets.

\textbf{S3 --- test.} A deterministic function runs the plan's test on the
counts: the exact binomial against the plan's $p_0$ (for a data-derived null,
against the chance sum over the retrieved denominator), Fisher's test for two
arms, modal-versus-runner-up for argmax; applies the floor; and returns the
verdict with both tails. Nothing is inferred at S3.

\subsection{Information: what the model is told}
\label{sec:frames}

The S1 prompt comes in two frames. The \emph{catalogue} frame hands the model
the table of claim types with their conditioning rule, null and test, so
planning reduces to classification and lookup; it is an answer-injected upper
bound. The \emph{toolbox} frame withholds that mapping and states every
convention by situation --- ``a report with two events tied for earliest has no
answer and is excluded''; ``order words are distance-blind; only wording that
says the two are adjacent conditions on consecutive stages''; ``association
wording compares the rate of $B$ among reports with $A$ against reports without
$A$'' --- together with a computed dataset card, the null sources and the test
definitions, and the model composes the plan. Zero-shot is the headline
condition: a worked plan is a mapping row.

The S2 prompt is where the plan is handed over, and it can be handed over in
two ways. As \emph{prose}: the plan's JSON and the full conventions text, from
which the SQL model must work out what the named primitive means. As a
\emph{contract}: the harness resolves the plan's claim type and supplies that
type's conditioning rule and arm plan in the prompt --- the same stipulation,
stated once, for the one primitive the plan names. The second is the
pipeline's design; the first is the ablation that shows why.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:verify}

Each stage has a bounded verify-and-repair loop that reads only the contract,
the stated conventions and the data --- never the gold. At S1 the plan is
checked for consistency with the conventions (a quantity against its
denominator, a null kind against its situation, a stated bar against the
wording, a valid test family) and re-asked once with the violations named. At
S2 a statement that the database rejects, an arm whose denominator equals its
numerator, an empty arm beside a full one, and --- when a plan exists --- rows
that violate the plan's primitive (co-existing reports in an order
denominator, ties in a first/last denominator, non-consecutive pairs in an
immediate denominator) trigger a re-ask, up to two rounds. The end-to-end
baselines receive the same loop with the checks that need no plan, which is
the control for what verification is worth without a contract. The planner
alone settles after one round; the SQL stage after two.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The experiments answer three questions, each on the test set and each with
the same five models in every model slot.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (information).} Can current language models verify
temporal--statistical claims end to end, and how much of what they achieve
depends on being told the corpus conventions?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (architecture).} Does handing the plan to retrieval as a
contract, with bounded verification, improve the verdicts of open models, and
where in the pipeline and in the claim space does the loss remain?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (evidence).} When a verdict is right, is it right for the
right reason, and which systems decide what the data cannot decide or abstain
on what they can?
\end{itemize}

\S\ref{sec:main}--\S\ref{sec:evidence} answer each in turn; the ablations
(\S\ref{sec:modular}) separate the stages and the repair budget, the case
studies (\S\ref{sec:cases}) cover single reports and paraphrase, and
\S\ref{sec:insufficient} analyses the \emph{insufficient} class.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

\emph{End-to-end sentence-to-SQL} (rows A and B) follows the single-query
text-to-SQL protocol of EHRSQL \cite{lee2022ehrsql}: the same SQL model
receives the schema and the claim and writes the per-report rows in one shot,
given the schema only (A) or the schema and the conventions (B); S3 is the
harness's, so these rows differ from our pipeline only in having no plan and
no contract. \emph{Agentic}: EHRAgent \cite{shi2024ehragent}, a code-writing
agent with error-driven retries over the same database, which runs its own
test and returns its own verdict (its paper used GPT-4 and GPT-3.5; we run it
with the open models and with GPT-5.6); VERITAS \cite{veritas2026}, run
through its hypothesis, statistics and interpretation agents with imaging
bypassed, given our exact-test functions and the conventions.
\emph{Counts-given judgment}: the model receives the gold counts and must
name the test and the verdict --- not a competitor but the floor of ``the
model does the statistics itself''. \emph{Ceilings}: the planner alone (gold
S2/S3) and the SQL stage alone (gold plan). Chance on the three-class cohort
tiers is $\approx 0.33$; a per-group majority guesser scores at most 0.37.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The verdict is scored as a three-class prediction:
accuracy, and precision, recall and F1 macro-averaged over \emph{supported},
\emph{refuted} and \emph{insufficient}, on the cohort tiers (the statistical
headline) and on the report tier (a reading task) separately. These four
numbers say how often and how evenly a system reaches the right label; they
cannot say whether it reached it for the right reason, because a verdict can
be right on the wrong evidence and wrong on the right evidence. Because every
stage has gold, four evidence measures are therefore reported with the
verdict, and they are what Figure~\ref{fig:families} shows. \emph{Plan EM} is exact match on the plan's
fields. \emph{Evidence F1} is the F1 between the set of reports a system
placed in an arm's denominator and the gold set, averaged over arms: it
measures whether the system counted the right reports, whatever it concluded
from them. \emph{Faithfulness} is the share of correct verdicts whose
evidence set is exactly the gold set, that is, the probability that a right
answer is right for the right reason. Two abstention measures complete the
picture: \emph{overclaim}, the share of gold-\emph{insufficient} claims
given a decisive verdict (deciding what the data cannot decide), and
\emph{underclaim}, the share of decisive claims answered
\emph{insufficient}. We also report the sub-type accuracy of abstentions, the
\emph{co-exist leak} (same-stage reports wrongly admitted to an order
denominator) and $p$-agreement with the exact computation.

\subsection{Experimental settings}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Splits.} The \emph{development set} is the 200-instance slice on
which every prompt decision was made (all three tiers, both forms, four
registers); it is a tuning set and no number from it enters the main table.
The \emph{test set} is 1{,}500 cohort- and multi-tier instances over the 17
cohort groups, 90 per group where the corpus allows it and every eligible
instance where it does not (four groups contribute 61--86), balanced over the
three verdicts (498 / 497 / 505) and fact-disjoint from the development set:
no instance in the test set shares a core with one that was tuned on. The
report tier is evaluated separately as a case study on 180 single-report
instances (20 per report group), because every capable system reads a single
report at 0.98--1.00 and the tier would only flatter weak rows. All test rows
use the \textsc{Original} register in claim form; prompts were frozen before
the first test record was run.

\paragraph{Models and compute.} Every system is run with each of five models
in every model slot: the 2024 open pair Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (planning) and
Qwen2.5-Coder-32B (SQL); the 2026 open models qwen3.8-27B and gemma4-31B, one
model for both stages, thinking off; and the frontier models Claude Opus~5.5
and GPT-5.6. Open models run fully resident on one RTX A5000 with an 8k
context; frontier models over their APIs with prompt caching. Verdict accuracies carry 95\% bootstrap
intervals over instances (1{,}000 resamples), about $\pm 0.02$ on the test set
and $\pm 0.01$ above 0.98.
Per-record cost and latency are in Appendix~\ref{app:cost}: a full frontier
column on the test set cost \$80--89 at list price with prompt caching,
EHRAgent with GPT-5.6 \$56, and the open models answer a test claim in
10--14 seconds on one workstation GPU.

\paragraph{Development set.} Every prompt decision was made on the
200-instance development set, one change at a time, and the analyses behind
the frozen configuration are reported there (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}): the
information-versus-architecture grid that separates what the prompt tells
the 2024 pair from what the decomposition adds, planning with the claim-type
mapping given versus withheld, the attribution of every denominator failure
to the rule it violated, and the effect of one missing stipulation in the
conventions and of the schema presentation.

\subsection{RQ1: what the stated conventions are worth}
\label{sec:main}

\paragraph{With the conventions stated, frontier models solve the task;
without them they do not.} Opus~5.5 and GPT-5.6 verify 1.000 and 0.984 of the
claims end to end once the corpus stipulations are in the prompt, with every
denominator exact. Remove the stipulations (row A) and the same models fall to
0.878 and 0.876 and get a fifth of their denominators wrong: they infer most
of the conventions from the schema and the wording and miss the rest. The
open models lose far more without them, 0.748 $\rightarrow$ 0.587 for
qwen3.8 and 0.859 $\rightarrow$ 0.635 for gemma4. The
benchmark is therefore not hard for a frontier model with the information; it
measures whether the information is used. Our pipeline adds nothing to such a
model (0.999), and its verifier costs it a little (0.993, 0.983): the plan
checks flag a few correct $n = k$ statements and the pipeline abstains rather
than guess.

\subsection{RQ2: what the contract adds, and where the loss remains}
\label{sec:families}

\paragraph{For open models the contract is what makes the verdict stand on the
right evidence.} The 2024 pair verifies 0.575 of claims end to end with the
conventions and 0.810 with the contract and the verifier; the 2026 models
0.748 $\rightarrow$ 0.872 (qwen3.8) and 0.859 $\rightarrow$ 0.981 (gemma4).
The verdict margin is $+0.12$ to $+0.24$. The evidence margin is larger and
does not shrink with model quality: with the contract 86--97\% of right
verdicts rest on the exact denominator against 40--86\% end to end, and
overclaim falls from 0.04--0.23 to 0.01--0.05. gemma4-31B with the
contract is within 0.02 of the frontier models at zero API cost, 14~seconds
per claim on one workstation GPU.

\subsection{RQ3: right verdicts on the right evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

\paragraph{The denominator is where systems fail, and the verdict hides it.}
The published agentic baseline reaches 0.605 with the 2024 Coder, within
0.03 of the end-to-end control, and 0.792 with GPT-5.6 against 0.984 for the
same model under the EHRSQL protocol with the conventions; its evidence
cannot be audited (it returns a verdict from its own code), and it decides
58\% and 15\% of the claims the data cannot decide
(\S\ref{sec:insufficient}); the multi-agent VERITAS, given the same conventions and our exact
tests, completes fewer than half its runs within a 10-minute cap per phase
and scores 0.402, abstaining on most decisive claims --- the opposite
failure. The end-to-end control with the 2024 Coder is right on 57\% of claims
and right for the right reason on 40\% of those: it admits same-stage reports
into order denominators, defines the eligible set as the supporting set, and
narrows presence splits to co-occurrence (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}). The
modular analysis (\S\ref{sec:modular}) shows the same thing from the other
side: handed the exact counts and asked only for the verdict, the open models
still pick the wrong test or read it wrong on a quarter to a third of claims,
so a correct verdict from them is not evidence of a correct computation.

\paragraph{The VERITAS subsample.} VERITAS's three agent phases take nine
minutes per claim, so it is run on 204 claims of the test set with the test
set's own stratification (12 per cohort group, four per verdict). On the same
204 claims our pipeline scores 0.804 (0.810 on all 1{,}500), the end-to-end
control 0.593 (0.575) and EHRAgent 0.559 (0.605), each inside its own
subsample interval (Appendix~\ref{app:subsample}).

\paragraph{Stage ceilings and the test stage.} The middle block of
Table~\ref{tab:main} fills one stage at a time with the model and leaves
the others at gold, so each row is the loss attributable to that stage
alone; the lower rows then compose the stages as the pipeline does, and every
row that has a verifier uses the pipeline's budget, two repair rounds at each
stage it fills. Planning is nearly solved by every model (0.917--0.993): with
the mapping withheld and the conventions stated, the plan is composed right on
at least nine claims in ten, and the gemma4 planner matches the frontier.
Retrieval is the 2024 pair's loss: its Coder with the gold plan scores
0.836, so the full pipeline's 0.761 is the planner's errors compounded on a
retrieval stage that was already losing a sixth of the claims. The 2026
models retrieve well with the gold plan (0.944 and 0.989), so for them the
remaining loss is the plan: gemma4 with the verifier (0.981) is within 0.01 of
both its ceilings, and qwen3.8 (0.872) sits 0.07 below its retrieval
ceiling and 0.05 below its planner ceiling. The test stage is a function and
the open models cannot compute it (0.61--0.78 when asked to judge from the
exact counts); the pipeline does not ask them to, and the row that replaces
S3 with an LLM verdict on the model's own counts shows what the same pipeline
loses when it does: gemma4 falls from 0.981 to 0.770, qwen3.8 from 0.872 to
0.568 and the 2024 pair from 0.810 to 0.512, with their own plans and retrieval
unchanged.

\paragraph{Prose versus contract.} The hand-off between S1 and S2 is the
decision the modular rows cannot see, because S2 alone is given the gold plan
already resolved. On the development set (Table~\ref{tab:grid}) the same
composed plan handed to S2 as prose with the conventions gives 0.573 for the
2024 pair, and 0.645 with two verify-and-repair rounds --- level with the
verified end-to-end model (0.660) --- while resolving the plan's named
primitive into its rule gives 0.855 and 0.865 with the same plan and the same
model. The decomposition pays only when the plan is a contract.

\paragraph{Repair rounds.} Figure~\ref{fig:rounds} runs our pipeline
with zero to three verify-and-repair rounds on the test set. For the open
models the first round does almost all of the work: $+0.13$ for qwen3.8 and
gemma4, with evidence F1 rising by 0.06--0.11 (Appendix~\ref{app:rounds}),
and the second and third
rounds add about 0.01 each. The 2024 pair gains little per round (0.761
$\rightarrow$ 0.814) because its Coder resubmits the same mistake when told
what was wrong. At the frontier every round costs 0.005--0.015: the checks
fire on statements that are already correct (a genuine $n = k$, an absent
event) and the pipeline turns a right verdict into an abstention. Records
that flip from right to wrong under repair flip almost entirely into
\emph{insufficient}; records that cannot be repaired (a quarter of those
flagged) are not executed. Two rounds is the operating point for every open
model; a model that does not need the verifier is not helped by it.

The three-decision verdict fails in two directions, deciding what the data
cannot decide and abstaining on what it can, and Table~\ref{tab:insufficient}
separates them with the cause of each. \emph{Overclaim is the agents' and the
end-to-end models' failure.} EHRAgent with the 2024 Coder decides 0.578 of the
claims the data cannot decide and 0.965 of the \emph{underpowered} ones: it
runs its own test and finds significance where the exact test finds none,
and with GPT-5.6 it still decides 0.145. The end-to-end control with the
2024 pair decides 0.234, again mostly on \emph{underpowered} claims (0.430),
where a denominator that admits the wrong reports moves the tail. Our
pipeline decides 0.010--0.046 of them with the open models and 0.004--0.020
at the frontier, because the exact test, not the model, does the abstaining.
On the 176 \emph{not-locatable} claims, where no report has the combination
and the right answer is an empty denominator, the end-to-end pair returns a
decisive verdict on 0.131 and EHRAgent on 0.364: the denominator was filled
with reports that do not qualify. Under the contract this falls to 0.011
for the pair and 0.000 for gemma4 and the frontier models; qwen3.8's 0.062
is its one weakness here. When our pipeline does abstain it names the right
sub-type on 0.871--0.970 of its abstentions with the open models and 0.996
or more at the frontier, against 0.791--0.893 for the end-to-end control.

\emph{Underclaim is the open models' larger loss, and its cause differs by
system.} The end-to-end pair abstains on 0.388 of the decisive claims, and
the prediction shows why: on 0.204 the denominator was testable but not the
gold set, on 0.108 it was below the floor; gemma4 end to end abstains on
0.182, half of it (0.098) queries the database rejected. Under the contract
the pair's 0.216 is mostly queries that still fail after two repairs (0.134,
the Coder resubmitting the same statement) and a wrong set on 0.047; qwen3.8
abstains on 0.066 and gemma4 on 0.023. The frontier models abstain on
0.009 and 0.011, almost all of it with the gold denominator (0.008, 0.010),
and every one of those is in H-V5: the plan reads ``most often first, in at
least half of the reports'' as an argmax claim and runs modal-versus-runner-up
on the right counts instead of the exact binomial against the stated bar.
The counts are exact and the test family is not, which is the one
composition the frontier planners miss and the whole of their loss on the
threshold family (\S\ref{sec:families}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{What the instrument shows.} The verdict is a poor proxy for the
reasoning it is supposed to certify: systems a few points apart on accuracy are
a factor of two apart on whether their evidence is right, and the end-to-end
systems' best gains came from fixing execution failures, not conditioning. The
stage-level gold is what makes this visible, and it is the dataset's main
contribution.

\paragraph{What the contract is for.} The reference pipeline is a simple
decomposition; its value is that each stage is checkable, and handing the plan
to the next stage as prose discards that value. What the results support is
stated conventions, a plan that is a contract, and bounded verification. A
frontier model that holds the conventions verifies the claims without the
contract (0.999 either way) and the verifier costs it a little; the contract
is what lets an open model, run locally on clinical data, reach the same
accuracy with an auditable denominator.

\paragraph{Conventions are dataset-specific.} Porting the framework to
another corpus means writing its conventions and primitive definitions, not
retraining anything. We have done this for one corpus; the cost of doing it
for another is not measured here.

\paragraph{Development and test.} Every prompt decision was made on the
200-instance development set, and the development numbers transfer to the
test set within 0.03 for every row we compared.

\paragraph{Floors and ceilings.} Decisive labels carry $\alpha$-rate false
positives by construction; exact tests are conservative, so the realised rate
is below nominal but not zero. The release is bounded by enumeration breadth
(the most frequent cohort terms), not by statistical power: three designed
groups (two three-event sequence groups and one co-occurrence threshold) have
only four to twelve cores in the corpus and are not in the roster. The 7B
tier is a capability floor: nothing in the framework helps a model that
cannot compose a plan.

\paragraph{No lexical variation.} Slot fillers are raw MedDRA terms and the
registers are templated; the paraphrase case study covers those four
registers.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sys{} turns a temporal--statistical claim into a chain of scored decisions ---
which reports count, what the null is, which test applies, what the evidence
was --- and shows that the first of those is where systems fail and the last is
what the verdict cannot see. On a 1{,}500-claim test set no prompt decision
was made on, frontier models verify the claims once the corpus conventions are
stated and not before; open models need the plan to be a contract between the
stages, and with it a 31B model run on one workstation GPU reaches 0.981 with
its evidence right 94\% of the time, against 0.859 and 86\% end to end.
Benchmarks of this kind should publish their conventions with their data, and
score the evidence with the answer.
