\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A growing class of language-model systems promises to test hypotheses
against data: given a claim and a dataset, they choose a statistical method,
run it and report a conclusion. Benchmarks for this ability
\cite{miao2026fisher,zhu2024statqa,zhu2026statabench} score the final answer
and hand the system the relevant data already assembled. In most real
settings that is the easy half. For the claim \emph{``after the Pfizer
vaccine, headache tends to precede fatigue''} over a corpus of annotated
adverse-event reports, a system must first decide \emph{which reports count}
(a report where the two events co-occur witnesses neither order), \emph{what
``no effect'' means} (a 50/50 split of the ordered reports) and \emph{which
test applies} (a one-sided exact binomial with a minimum-count floor). Each
decision is a stipulation about what the sentence means for this corpus,
each can be wrong independently, and a verdict can be right while all of
them are wrong.

This paper makes that chain of decisions the object of evaluation. \sys{}
(Staged Temporal-statistical Reasoning Assessment) is a benchmark and
evaluation framework for temporal--statistical claims over MedTempo, a corpus
of 3{,}166 vaccine adverse-event reports whose clinical course is annotated
as a \emph{leveled order} of events. Every instance carries its gold verdict
(\emph{supported}, \emph{refuted} or a sub-typed \emph{insufficient}) and the
gold of every stage that produced it: the structured plan, the exact report
sets in the denominator and numerator, and the exact $p$-values, all
computed mechanically from the annotations and reproduced by a deterministic
oracle. We pair the benchmark with an instrumented reference pipeline,
\art{} (articulate, retrieve, test): a stage that articulates the claim as a
plan, a retrieval stage and a deterministic test stage, each with an output
contract and a bounded verify-and-repair loop. Its stages can be filled with
gold, its prompts can state or withhold the corpus \emph{codebook} (the
stipulations about how the annotations are to be read, written as text), and
its plan can be handed on either as prose or as a \emph{contract}, in which
the harness expands each primitive the plan names into that primitive's
exact rule. That separates what end-to-end evaluation conflates, the
\emph{information} a system is told and the \emph{architecture} it is given.

On a 1{,}500-claim test set that no prompt decision was made on, with five
models from the 2024 open generation to the 2026 frontier, three findings
answer three questions: the codebook is the information the task measures,
and frontier models solve the task once they have it; for open models the
contract is what makes the verdict stand on the right evidence, carrying a
31B model to within 0.02 of the frontier at zero API cost; and the
denominator is where systems fail, which verdict accuracy hides. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A benchmark with gold at every stage}: temporal--statistical
claims in 26 groups and seven families over three difficulty levels, released
as a development set, a fact-disjoint test set and a single-report case
study, with structured plans, exact evidence sets, exact $p$-values and a
three-decision verdict with sub-typed abstention (\S\ref{sec:dataset}).
\item \textbf{A generation process that makes the codebook part of the
task}: claims enumerated from the annotated timelines by templates, gold
computed mechanically, screened against permutation nulls and validated by
invariants and an oracle, and the stipulations the labels depend on shipped
as text any system can be given (\S\ref{sec:dataset}).
\item \textbf{A staged verification method}, \art{}, whose plan is handed to
retrieval as a contract resolved by the harness, with bounded repair at each
stage and a deterministic exact test, so that any stage can be set to gold
and information and architecture are separately controllable
(\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A study of five models and two agentic systems} under three
research questions, with stage and repair-budget ablations, case studies and
an error analysis of the \emph{insufficient} class
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}, \S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Statistical reasoning benchmarks.} StatQA \cite{zhu2024statqa}
scores whether a model knows which test applies, StatFormBench
\cite{wang2026statformbench} the formulation of a test family, and StatABench
\cite{zhu2026statabench} the final answer with fixed tool functions. P-Bench
and Fisher-R1 \cite{miao2026fisher} come closest to our setting: an agent
receives a hypothesis with its null stated, a CSV of the relevant data and a
glossary, writes R to compute a $p$-value, and is scored on the decision and
the $p$-value. In all of these the data are already the relevant subset and
the null is given or trivial; the step we score first, deciding which records
a statistic is computed over, has no counterpart, and no intermediate stage
has gold.

\paragraph{Hypothesis-testing agents.} VERITAS \cite{veritas2026} is a
multi-agent co-scientist with a deterministic evidence classifier, built for
image-derived cohorts; LATCH \cite{gim2026latch} runs LLM-chosen analyses
through deterministic R pipelines on EHR data with the method given;
DiscoveryBench and BLADE \cite{majumder2025discoverybench,gu2024blade}
evaluate open-ended discovery agents against rubrics. They share our
plan-then-execute shape and our insistence on auditable computation, but
none scores the conditioning of the evidence and none has hypotheses about
the order of events within a record. We run two of them, EHRAgent
\cite{shi2024ehragent} and VERITAS, as baselines.

\paragraph{Clinical text-to-SQL and timelines.} EHRSQL \cite{lee2022ehrsql}
scores one query per question with an abstention option, and EHRAgent
\cite{shi2024ehragent} solves EHR questions with a code-writing agent and
error-driven retries; our Straight-to-SQL baselines (\S\ref{sec:baselines})
follow the EHRSQL protocol over our schema. TIMER \cite{timer2025} and CRAFT
\cite{craft2026} extract and refine clinical timelines from narratives, and
MedTempo, the corpus CRAFT introduced, is the expert-annotated set of such
timelines we build on: a data source, not a competitor. We start where
extraction ends.

\paragraph{Positioning.} Table~\ref{tab:related-concise} compares the closest
work. A plan-then-execute loop with a deterministic statistics layer (LATCH)
and ``hypothesis and data in, $p$ out'' (P-Bench) are not new; what is new
is a model-formulated null executed deterministically with gold at every
stage, a scored eligibility rule, and claims about event order with the
conditioning semantics those claims carry.

\section{The \sys{} Benchmark}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Task}
\label{sec:task}


\paragraph{Input and output.} A \emph{record} is a claim in natural language
--- a declarative sentence in one of four registers --- about a
\emph{cohort} (the reports of one vaccine), a \emph{contrast} of two cohorts, or
one named \emph{report}; and the annotations: for every report, its vaccine
and the stage at which each event term is first mentioned. The system must
return a verdict in $\{\emph{supported}, \emph{refuted}, \emph{insufficient}\}$,
the last sub-typed as \emph{not-locatable} (no report qualifies),
\emph{sparse} (too few qualify) or \emph{underpowered} (enough qualify, neither
tail significant). It never sees report text.

\paragraph{What a verdict rests on.} For a cohort-tier claim the gold is
computed in three steps that are themselves released: (1) a \emph{plan} ---
the events named, the quantity compared, the \emph{denominator primitive}
(which reports count), the number of arms, the null (a fixed proportion, a bar
stated in the text, a rate derived from the data, or equality of two arms), the
tail and the test family; (2) the \emph{evidence} --- the identifiers of the
reports in each arm's denominator and numerator; (3) the exact test with a
per-family minimum-count floor and $\alpha = 0.05$ one-sided in the claim's
direction. \emph{Refuted} requires significance in the opposite direction, never
``failed to prove''. A report-tier claim is a single deterministic observation
over one report and carries no test.

\paragraph{The codebook is part of the task.} Whether a report with both
events at the same stage counts for ``precedes'', what ``typically'' means,
whether a tie for ``first'' leaves the denominator, what ``more often than their
separate rates would predict'' compares --- these are stipulations about the
corpus, not facts about the sentence, and a system that was never told them
cannot infer them. The benchmark therefore ships these stipulations as a
\emph{codebook}, in the sense of an annotation codebook: a situation-phrased
text that any system may be given (\S\ref{sec:frames}); the experiments
treat ``codebook given'' as a controlled condition. The
claim-type \emph{mapping} --- which rule and which test go with which wording
--- is what we withhold: a system is scored on composing it.


\subsection{Source corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

MedTempo~\cite{craft2026} is a corpus of 3{,}166 VAERS adverse-event
reports for three COVID-19 vaccines, annotated with a \emph{leveled order}: a
single totally ordered chain of stages in which each stage holds the
MedDRA-coded events reported at that point of the clinical course (1{,}164
Janssen, 1{,}019 Pfizer and 983 Moderna reports; 1{,}660 distinct event
terms; 7.2 events and 3.4 stages per report on average). Two annotation rules
are load-bearing: when a term recurs, its \emph{first} mention
fixes its stage; events at one stage are temporally indistinguishable
(\emph{co-exist}). The annotation, not the narrative, is both the benchmark's
ground truth and the systems' input.

\subsection{Hypothesis space}
\label{sec:space}

Twenty-six template groups span seven claim families at three scopes
(Table~\ref{tab:roster}). Four families concern order and co-occurrence ---
\emph{precedes}, \emph{co-occurs}, strict \emph{sequence} of three events,
\emph{first/last} among candidates --- and three, added to diversify the null,
concern \emph{position} (an event in the earlier half of a timeline),
\emph{proximity} (two events at consecutive stages) and \emph{threshold} (a
rate at least a stated bar). Claims come at three \emph{levels of
difficulty}, ordered by what a claim asks of a reader, not by how systems
score on it. \emph{Easy} (the report tier) asks about one named report: the
verdict is a deterministic reading of its timeline (did $A$ precede $B$ in
this report), there is no null and no test, and \emph{insufficient} does not
arise (9 groups). \emph{Medium} (the cohort tier) asks about the reports of
one vaccine: a denominator has to be assembled under the family's rule, a
null stated, and an exact test run with a floor on the count, so all three
verdicts are possible (16 groups). \emph{Hard} (the two-cohort tier)
contrasts two vaccines' cohorts: two denominators, two rates, and a
two-sample exact test (1 group). Within the cohort tier, three groups are
themselves two-arm contrasts: whether $B$ follows $A$ more often than it follows $C$; whether $A$
precedes $B$ more often than $C$ precedes $D$; and whether $A$ and $B$ occur
together more often than their separate rates predict (association). Each
group is realised as a declarative claim in four registers (a canonical
\textsc{Original} plus \textsc{Research}, \textsc{Media} and
\textsc{Clinical}) by mechanical slot-filling with no model-generated
rewriting; answers are \textsc{Bool} or \textsc{Choice}.

\paragraph{Five kinds of null.} Order claims test a proportion against the
symmetry value $0.5$; argmax claims (``most common first'', ``most common
ordering'') test the named cell against its strongest competitor; two-arm
claims test the equality of two rates; threshold claims read their null off
the text (``in at least two thirds''); and the position and proximity-rate
claims use a null \emph{derived from the data} --- the structural early-rate of
the denominator reports, or the chance $2/L$ that two events of an $L$-stage
report are adjacent --- which no lookup table can supply. The last two kinds
are what make ``formulate the null'' a scored capability rather than a
classification.

\subsection{Gold labels}
\label{sec:gold}

\paragraph{Conditioning.} Each family fixes its denominator, and these
distinctions are the substantive content of the task. \emph{Precedes} counts
only reports where both events occupy distinct stages. \emph{Co-occurs} counts
all reports containing both, because co-existence is the event of interest.
\emph{Sequence} requires all three events at distinct stages. \emph{First/last}
requires every candidate present and drops tie reports entirely. Association
conditions on presence alone, comparing $P(B \mid A)$ against $P(B \mid \neg
A)$ over the whole cohort. \emph{Immediate} order counts reports where the two
events are at consecutive occupied stages; \emph{position} drops single-stage
reports and events on the exact middle stage.

\paragraph{Tests.} All tests are exact, and Table~\ref{tab:tests} lists
them by claim shape: a one-sided exact binomial against $p_0$ for majority,
bar and data-derived nulls; modal-versus-runner-up, an exact binomial of the
named cell against its strongest competitor, for argmax claims; Fisher's
exact test \cite{fisher1935design} for two-arm and two-cohort contrasts. No
$\chi^2$ approximation appears; the sole gate is a minimum denominator of 10,
applied to both arms where two exist. Each instance releases both one-sided
$p$-values, the Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927} and Cohen's $h$
\cite{cohen1988} against the null actually used.

\paragraph{The three-decision rule and oriented statistics.} A claim is
\emph{supported} only when its own one-sided tail is significant at
$\alpha = 0.05$, \emph{refuted} only when the opposite tail is, and otherwise
\emph{insufficient} (\emph{underpowered}, \emph{sparse}: $0 < n <$ floor, or
\emph{not-locatable}: $n = 0$). Both tails are computed for every instance.
Every released statistic --- both $p$-values, the Wilson interval, Cohen's $h$
against the null actually used --- describes the claim as that instance asserts
it.

\subsection{Instance realisation and composition}
\label{sec:realization}

Every decisive core is posed \emph{both ways}: oriented with its effect
(\emph{supported}) and against it (\emph{refuted}), a mirror pair over
identical evidence, so a system answering \emph{supported} to both directions
of one fact is caught immediately. Direction-asking groups re-orient by
swapping slots; symmetric claims re-orient by authored negation, written into
the template rather than derived; threshold claims are tested per orientation
(both directions can be refuted) and carry no mirror. Each cohort group ships
equal thirds --- supported, refuted, insufficient --- balanced \emph{per group}
because template wording is fixed per group and a skewed group would teach a
text-shape-to-label shortcut; the insufficient third is spread over its
sub-types. \emph{Not-locatable} instances are minted deliberately from real
cohort vocabulary whose joint count is zero, so the claim appears answerable and
only querying the annotations reveals the empty denominator. Groups are capped
at 600 instances (200 per class) by a seeded draw that keeps mirror pairs
together; report groups ship two classes of 200.

\subsection{Validity screens}
\label{sec:screens}

No group ships on plausibility. Each is screened against a permutation null
\cite{good2005permutation} matched to its claim shape at 2{,}000 rounds: a \emph{level shuffle}
(term-to-stage assignment permuted within each report, preserving report size,
stage-size multiset, term frequencies and co-occurrence) for order, position
and proximity groups; a degree-preserving \emph{edge swap} for association; a
\emph{label shuffle} for two-cohort contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:screens}). The
screening history is itself a result: seven candidate two-cohort shapes were
tested and one survived; a majority statistic was replaced after the screen
showed its text and its test answered different questions; and a rank-test
track was retired when the Mann--Whitney approximation proved anti-conservative
on tie-heavy ordinal data (${\sim}11\%$ of cores decisive under shuffled labels
at a nominal 5\%).

\subsection{Release, splits and leakage}
\label{sec:release}

\paragraph{Two files.} The questions file carries one row per rendering and
only the input fields (identifier, group, level, answer type, register,
text, options, cohort); the answers file carries one row per instance with the
gold verdict and sub-type, the oriented counts, both $p$-values, the test, the
floor, interval and effect size, and the \emph{evidence identifiers}. The writer
refuses to emit a gold field into the questions file. A group-independent
\texttt{fact\_id} keys every split, because answer-type twins pose the same
fact under two groups.

\paragraph{Pseudonymised identifiers.} Every report identifier a system can
see --- in the claim texts, in the released evidence and in the database --- is
a keyed permutation of the public VAERS identifier. VAERS is public and in
pretraining corpora; removing the lookup key removes the shortcut without
touching the annotations.

\paragraph{Splits.} The release is an evaluation benchmark with no training
split: a \emph{development set} of 200 instances (a seeded, class-balanced
draw over every group and tier, in all four registers) on
which every prompt and design decision in this paper was made, and a
\emph{test set} of 1{,}500 cohort- and multi-tier instances in claim form,
90 per group where the corpus allows it and every eligible instance where it
does not, class-balanced, mirror pairs and facts kept together, and
fact-disjoint from the development set. A further 180 single-report instances
(20 per report group) form a case study rather than part of the test set,
because that tier is read at 0.98--1.00 by every capable system. The full
enumerated pool (Table~\ref{tab:totals}) is released as supplementary
material with the generator, so larger or differently balanced draws can be
made with the same recipe. The release also carries a question-form rendering
of every record; this paper presents and studies the claim form only. The
test set was run once, after the prompts were frozen.

\paragraph{Validation.} Six families of mechanical invariants --- evidence
arithmetic, set algebra, per-family conditioning re-derived from the leveled
orders, direction, verdict-against-tails including the floor, and independent
recomputation through a separate database path --- are checked over the whole
enumerated universe (56{,}138 instances). A deterministic oracle (reverse
template parser into the reference SQL compiler) reproduces every gold label of
the release: the harness is correct, whatever the text entails.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:framework}
\label{sec:method}

Our reference pipeline, \art{}, verifies a claim in three stages, articulate,
retrieve and test, and is built so that each stage can be scored against its gold, filled
with gold, or told more or less about the corpus. This section gives the
stage contracts, the two prompt frames that control what the model is told,
and the verification loop; the systems it is compared against are in
\S\ref{sec:baselines}.

\subsection{Stage contracts}
\label{sec:contracts}

The reference pipeline decomposes verification into three stages with explicit
output contracts (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\textbf{S1 --- articulate.} From the record (text, level, cohort) the model composes
a plan: \texttt{events}, \texttt{quantity}, \texttt{denominator} (a named
primitive: \emph{ordered\_pair}, \emph{both\_present},
\emph{all\_present\_unique\_first}, \emph{consecutive\_pair},
\emph{present\_offmid}, \emph{presence\_split}, \emph{single\_report}, \ldots),
\texttt{arms}, \texttt{null\_kind} with $p_0$ or a reference population,
\texttt{tail}, \texttt{test\_family} and a rule for the floor. The harness
derives the claim type from the plan by reverse lookup over the plan's fields;
a plan that names no known shape is \emph{underdetermined} and scored as
insufficient. Two normalisation folds are applied before lookup: a
consecutive-pair denominator implies the immediate-order quantity, and a
stated or derived null on a shape with no such twin keeps the shape and costs
one field.

\textbf{S2 --- retrieve.} For each arm the model writes one SQL statement over
the annotation database (two tables, two views) that returns \emph{one row per
report} with four flags: in the denominator, in the numerator, excluded, and an
optional cell label for argmax claims. The harness executes it in a read-only
sandbox and aggregates the rows into $(n, k)$ per arm together with the
identifier sets, so the evidence is auditable against the gold sets.

\textbf{S3 --- test.} A deterministic function runs the plan's test on the
counts (Table~\ref{tab:tests}): the exact binomial against the plan's $p_0$
(for a data-derived null, against the chance sum over the retrieved
denominator), Fisher's test for two arms, modal-versus-runner-up for argmax;
applies the floor; and returns the verdict with both tails. Nothing is
inferred at S3.

\subsection{Information: what the model is told}
\label{sec:frames}

The S1 prompt comes in two frames. The \emph{catalogue} frame hands the model
the table of claim types with their conditioning rule, null and test, so
planning reduces to classification and lookup; it is an answer-injected upper
bound. The \emph{toolbox} frame withholds that mapping and states the
codebook by situation --- ``a report with two events tied for earliest has no
answer and is excluded''; ``order words are distance-blind; only wording that
says the two are adjacent conditions on consecutive stages''; ``association
wording compares the rate of $B$ among reports with $A$ against reports without
$A$'' --- together with a computed dataset card, the null sources and the test
definitions, and the model composes the plan. Zero-shot is the headline
condition: a worked plan is a mapping row.

The S2 prompt is where the plan is handed over, and it can be handed over in
two ways. As \emph{prose}: the plan's JSON and the full codebook, from
which the SQL model must work out what the named primitive means. As a
\emph{contract}: the harness resolves the plan's claim type and supplies that
type's conditioning rule and arm plan in the prompt --- the same stipulation,
stated once, for the one primitive the plan names. The second is the
pipeline's design; the first is the ablation that shows why.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:verify}

Each stage has a bounded verify-and-repair loop that reads only the contract,
the codebook and the data --- never the gold. At S1 the plan is
checked for consistency with the codebook (a quantity against its
denominator, a null kind against its situation, a stated bar against the
wording, a valid test family) and re-asked once with the violations named. At
S2 a statement that the database rejects, an arm whose denominator equals its
numerator, an empty arm beside a full one, and --- when a plan exists --- rows
that violate the plan's primitive (co-existing reports in an order
denominator, ties in a first/last denominator, non-consecutive pairs in an
immediate denominator) trigger a re-ask, up to two rounds. The Straight-to-SQL
baselines receive the same loop with the checks that need no plan, which is
the control for what verification is worth without a contract. The planner
alone settles after one round; the SQL stage after two.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The experiments answer three questions, each on the test set and each with
the same five models in every model slot.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} How well do current language models verify these claims
end to end, and how much does giving them the corpus codebook change the
result?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} What does the staged pipeline add over Straight-to-SQL
verification for each model, and which stage and which claim families
account for the errors that remain?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Do correct verdicts rest on correct evidence, and how
often do systems decide claims the data cannot decide, or abstain on claims
it can?
\end{itemize}

\S\ref{sec:main}--\S\ref{sec:evidence} answer each in turn; the ablations
(\S\ref{sec:modular}) separate the stages and the repair budget, the case
studies (\S\ref{sec:cases}) cover single reports and paraphrase, and
\S\ref{sec:insufficient} analyses the \emph{insufficient} class.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Three published systems are adapted to the benchmark, and all rows run on
the same 1{,}500 claims, database and codebook text. \emph{EHRSQL}
\cite{lee2022ehrsql} is the single-query text-to-SQL protocol over our
schema, which we call Straight-to-SQL in the text: one prompt per claim to
the SQL model, carrying the schema as a column listing and the row contract
(one row per report with denominator, numerator and excluded flags), with or
without the codebook (\emph{EHRSQL + codebook}); the query runs in the
read-only sandbox and the harness runs the exact test, so these rows differ
from our pipeline only in having no plan, no contract and no repair.
\emph{EHRAgent} \cite{shi2024ehragent} runs the authors' code over the same
database in its end-to-end variant: it writes and executes its own code with
error-driven retries (up to twelve) and four demonstrations, runs its own
test and returns a bare verdict. Its paper used GPT-4 and GPT-3.5; we run the
2024 Coder, qwen3.8 and GPT-5.6, all through the code-block path.
\emph{VERITAS} \cite{veritas2026} runs its hypothesis, statistics and
interpretation agents as released with imaging bypassed, our exact-test
functions bound in place of its statistics library and the codebook as the
data description; each phase is capped at ten minutes, and at nine minutes
per claim it is run with the Coder on a stratified 204-claim subsample
(Appendix~\ref{app:subsample}).

The ablations isolate parts of \art{}. \emph{Articulate alone} fills the plan
with the model (one repair round) and retrieval and test with gold;
\emph{Retrieve alone} hands the model the gold plan already resolved into its
contract (two repair rounds) and runs the exact test on its counts.
\emph{LLM as statistician} gives the model the gold counts and the test
definitions and asks it to choose the test and return the verdict, the floor
of ``the model does the statistics''; \emph{\art{} + LLM as statistician}
asks the same on the pipeline's own counts, so it differs from \art{} only
in the test stage. \emph{\art{} without verifier} removes the repair loops.
Chance on the three-class cohort
tiers is $\approx 0.33$; a per-group majority guesser scores at most 0.37.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The verdict is scored as a three-class prediction:
accuracy, and precision, recall and F1 macro-averaged over \emph{supported},
\emph{refuted} and \emph{insufficient}, on the cohort tiers (the statistical
headline) and on the report tier (a reading task) separately. These four
numbers say how often and how evenly a system reaches the right label; they
cannot say whether it reached it for the right reason, because a verdict can
be right on the wrong evidence and wrong on the right evidence. Because every
stage has gold, four evidence measures are therefore reported with the
verdict, and they are what Figure~\ref{fig:families} shows. \emph{Plan EM} is exact match on the plan's
fields. \emph{Evidence F1} is the F1 between the set of reports a system
placed in an arm's denominator and the gold set, averaged over arms: it
measures whether the system counted the right reports, whatever it concluded
from them. \emph{Faithfulness} is the share of correct verdicts whose
evidence set is exactly the gold set, that is, the probability that a right
answer is right for the right reason. Two abstention measures complete the
picture: \emph{overclaim}, the share of gold-\emph{insufficient} claims
given a decisive verdict (deciding what the data cannot decide), and
\emph{underclaim}, the share of decisive claims answered
\emph{insufficient}. We also report the sub-type accuracy of abstentions, the
\emph{co-exist leak} (same-stage reports wrongly admitted to an order
denominator) and $p$-agreement with the exact computation.

\subsection{Experimental settings}
\label{sec:setup}

The splits are those of \S\ref{sec:release}: the 200-instance development
set, on which every prompt decision was made and from which no number enters
the main table; the 1{,}500-claim test set, run once after the prompts were
frozen, in the \textsc{Original} register and claim form (the paraphrase
case study reruns it in the three other registers); and the 180-claim
report case study, kept apart because every capable system reads a single
report at 0.98--1.00. Every system is run with each of five models in every
model slot: the 2024 open pair Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (planning) and
Qwen2.5-Coder-32B (SQL) \cite{qwen2025qwen25}; the 2026 open models
qwen3.8-27B \cite{qwen2026qwen38} and gemma4-31B \cite{gemma2026gemma4}, one
model for both stages, thinking off; and the frontier models Claude Opus~5.5
\cite{anthropic2026opus55} and GPT-5.6 \cite{openai2026gpt56}. Open models run fully resident on one RTX A5000 with an 8k
context, frontier models over their APIs with prompt caching. Verdict
accuracies carry 95\% bootstrap intervals \cite{efron1993bootstrap} over
instances (1{,}000 resamples),
about $\pm 0.02$ on the test set and $\pm 0.01$ above 0.98. Per-record cost
and latency are in Appendix~\ref{app:cost}; a frontier column on the test set
cost \$80--89 at list price, and the open models answer a claim in 10--14
seconds on one workstation GPU. The development-set analyses behind the
frozen configuration are in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}, and the full
information-versus-architecture grid is released with the code.

\subsection{RQ1: Straight-to-SQL verification and the codebook}
\label{sec:main}

\paragraph{With the codebook, frontier models solve the task; without it
they do not.} Given the codebook, Opus~5.5 and GPT-5.6 verify essentially
every claim end to end with every denominator exact. Without it the same
models lose a tenth of their verdicts and a fifth of their denominators: they
infer most of the stipulations from the schema and the wording and miss the
rest. The open models lose far more without it. The benchmark is therefore
not hard for a frontier model that has the information; it measures whether
the information is used. \art{} adds nothing to such a model, and its
verifier costs it a little, because the plan checks flag a few correct
$n = k$ statements and the pipeline abstains rather than guess.

\subsection{RQ2: the staged pipeline and where errors remain}
\label{sec:families}

\paragraph{What the contract adds depends on how much the model left on the
table.} The frontier models have no verdict headroom with the codebook: they
score the same under \art{} as end to end, and the verifier costs them a
little, because its checks flag a few correct $n = k$ statements and the
pipeline abstains rather than guess. What the pipeline gives them is not
accuracy but an auditable trail: a plan whose fields are scored, a report set
per arm, and a test the harness ran, which the one-prompt query does not
produce even when it is right. For the open models the contract is what
makes the verdict stand on the right evidence: with the contract and the
verifier every open model gains between 0.12 and 0.24 in verdict accuracy
over the Straight-to-SQL control, and gemma4-31B lands within 0.02 of the
frontier at zero API cost. The evidence margin is larger and does not shrink
with model quality: under the contract 86--97\% of right verdicts rest on the
exact denominator, against 40--86\% end to end, and overclaim falls by a
factor of four or more. The contract does not make the models better at SQL;
it removes the interpretation step on which they were failing.

\subsection{RQ3: the evidence behind the verdicts}
\label{sec:evidence}

\paragraph{The denominator is where systems fail, and the verdict hides it.}
The published agentic baseline is within 0.03 of the Straight-to-SQL control
with the 2024 Coder and below \art{} with every newer model, and its evidence
cannot be audited: it returns a verdict from its own code, and with the
Coder it decides more than half of the claims the data cannot decide
(\S\ref{sec:insufficient}). VERITAS, given the same codebook and our exact
tests, shows the opposite failure, completing fewer than half its runs
within a ten-minute cap per phase and abstaining on most decisive claims. The
Straight-to-SQL control with the 2024 Coder is right on 57\% of claims and
right for the right reason on only 40\% of those: it admits same-stage
reports into order denominators, defines the eligible set as the supporting
set, and narrows presence splits to co-occurrence
(Table~\ref{tab:attribution}). The LLM-as-statistician rows show the same
thing from the other side: handed the exact counts, the open models still
return the wrong verdict on a quarter to a third of claims, so a correct
verdict from them is not evidence of a correct computation. VERITAS's nine
minutes per claim confine it to a 204-claim stratified subsample of the test
set; every other system scores within its own interval on those claims
(Appendix~\ref{app:subsample}).

\paragraph{Stage ceilings and the test stage.} The stage ceilings in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} fill one stage at a time with the model and leaves the
others at gold, so each row is the loss attributable to that stage alone.
Planning is nearly solved by every model: with the mapping withheld and the
codebook given, the plan is composed right on at least nine claims in ten,
and the gemma4 planner matches the frontier. Retrieval is where the 2024
pair loses, its Coder dropping a sixth of the claims even with the gold
plan, so the full pipeline compounds planner errors on a stage that was
already failing. The 2026 models retrieve well with the gold plan, so their
remaining loss is the plan, and gemma4 with the verifier sits within 0.01 of
both its ceilings. The test stage is a function the open models cannot
compute: as the statistician on the exact counts an LLM is wrong on a quarter
to a third of claims, and the row that makes the LLM the statistician on
its own counts shows the same pipeline losing 0.2 to 0.3 when it is asked to.

\paragraph{Prose versus contract.} The hand-off between S1 and S2 is what the
ceiling rows cannot see, because Retrieve alone receives the gold plan already
resolved. On the development set the same composed plan handed to S2 as
prose with the codebook scores below the verified Straight-to-SQL model even
after two repair rounds, while resolving the plan's named primitive into its
rule adds about 0.3 with the same plan and the same model. The decomposition
pays only when the plan is a contract.

\paragraph{Repair rounds.} Figure~\ref{fig:rounds} runs the pipeline with
zero to three verify-and-repair rounds. For the 2026 open models the first
round does almost all of the work, about $+0.13$ in accuracy with evidence
F1 rising with it (Appendix~\ref{app:rounds}), and the next two rounds add a
few thousandths; the 2024 pair gains little per round because its Coder
resubmits the same mistake when told what was wrong. At the frontier every
round costs a little, since the checks fire on statements that are already
correct and the pipeline turns a right verdict into an abstention. Two rounds
is the operating point for every open model; a model that does not need the
verifier is not helped by it.

The three-decision verdict fails in two directions, deciding what the data
cannot decide and abstaining on what it can, and Table~\ref{tab:insufficient}
separates them with the cause of each. \emph{Overclaim is the agents' and the
one-prompt models' failure.} EHRAgent with the 2024 Coder decides more than
half of the claims the data cannot decide and nearly all of the
\emph{underpowered} ones, because it runs its own test and finds
significance where the exact test finds none; its newer models decide far
fewer. The Straight-to-SQL control overclaims mostly on \emph{underpowered}
claims, where a denominator that admits the wrong reports moves the tail.
Our pipeline overclaims on a few percent at most, because the exact test,
not the model, does the abstaining. On the \emph{not-locatable} claims,
where no report has the combination and the right answer is an empty
denominator, the one-prompt pair and EHRAgent return decisive verdicts on a
tenth to a third of claims by filling the denominator with reports that do
not qualify; under the contract this all but disappears, qwen3.8 being the
one open model that still does it occasionally. When our pipeline abstains
it names the right sub-type far more often than the control does.

\emph{Underclaim is the open models' larger loss, and its cause differs by
system.} The one-prompt pair abstains on nearly four decisive claims in ten,
mostly because its denominator was testable but not the gold set or fell
below the floor; gemma4 end to end abstains mainly on queries the database
rejected. EHRAgent moves the other way as its model improves, from almost
never abstaining with the Coder to abstaining on a fifth of the decisive
claims with qwen3.8 and GPT-5.6: the stronger agents trade overclaim for
underclaim rather than for correct verdicts. Under the contract the pair's
remaining abstentions are mostly queries that still fail after two repairs,
the Coder resubmitting the same statement, and the 2026 models abstain on a
few percent. The frontier models' few abstentions all come with the gold
denominator and all fall in one group, H-V5: the plan reads ``most often
first, in at least half of the reports'' as an argmax claim and runs
modal-versus-runner-up on the right counts instead of the exact binomial
against the stated bar. The counts are exact and the test family is not,
which is the one composition the frontier planners miss and the whole of
their loss on the threshold family (\S\ref{sec:families}).

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sys{} turns a temporal--statistical claim into a chain of scored decisions,
which reports count, what the null is, which test applies and what the
evidence was, and shows that the first of those is where systems fail and
the last is what the verdict cannot see: systems a few points apart on
accuracy are a factor of two apart on whether their evidence is right. On a
1{,}500-claim test set no prompt decision was made on, frontier models verify
the claims once the corpus codebook is stated and not before; open models
need the plan to be a contract between the stages, and with it a 31B model
run on one workstation GPU reaches the frontier's accuracy with an auditable
denominator. The reference pipeline is a simple decomposition whose value is
that each stage is checkable; handing the plan on as prose discards that
value, and a model that does not need the verifier is not helped by it.

Three limits bound the claims. The codebook is corpus-specific: porting the
framework means writing another corpus's codebook and primitive definitions,
a cost we have paid for one corpus and not measured for another. Decisive
labels carry $\alpha$-rate false positives by construction, and the release
is bounded by enumeration breadth rather than statistical power, three
designed groups having too few cores to ship. Slot fillers are raw MedDRA
terms and the registers are templated, so lexical variation is limited to
the paraphrase case study, and a 7B model that cannot compose a plan is not
helped by anything in the framework. Benchmarks of this kind should publish
their codebook with their data, and score the evidence with the answer.
